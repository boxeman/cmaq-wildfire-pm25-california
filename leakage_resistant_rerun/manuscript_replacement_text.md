## Methods — leakage-resistant Random Forest analysis

We modelled daily CMAQ total PM₂.₅ error, defined as CMAQ total minus observed PM₂.₅. To prevent target leakage, neither observed PM₂.₅ nor total CMAQ PM₂.₅ was included as a predictor. Because simultaneous inclusion of fire-only and no-fire CMAQ PM₂.₅ would reconstruct total CMAQ PM₂.₅, the primary model retained fire-only PM₂.₅ and excluded the no-fire component. The remaining predictors were latitude, longitude, year, month, day of year, distance to fire, a distance-missing indicator, and the daily observation count. Observed concentration-regime labels, fire names, and fire-window tags were excluded from training and used only for post hoc evaluation.

Observations were grouped by monitoring site and year. A deterministic 20% of the 2,139 site-year groups was reserved as an untouched test partition, leaving the other groups for model development. Five-fold GroupKFold cross-validation was conducted within the training partition. Missing distance-to-fire values were median-imputed using the relevant training data only; their missingness indicator was retained. The Random Forest used 160 trees, maximum depth 24, minimum terminal-node size 10, 70% of predictors considered per split, and random seed 20260905. Performance was assessed with the coefficient of determination (R²), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), Pearson correlation reported separately, and mean residual defined as predicted minus actual error. Test-set permutation importance used 10 repeated permutations on a fixed 20,000-row test sample. Accumulated local effects were used instead of partial dependence to reduce distortion from correlated predictors.

The primary QC analysis retained 187,051 otherwise-valid records and quarantined, without capping, 18 records with total CMAQ PM₂.₅ above 557 µg m⁻³ pending verification against the raw CMAQ source. A prespecified sensitivity analysis restored those 18 records. Additional comparisons included a training-mean predictor, linear regression with the same predictors, a fire-only Random Forest, and a model replacing the fire-only component with the no-fire component.

## Results — leakage-resistant model

The leakage-resistant Random Forest explained 71.1% of the variation in CMAQ error in the untouched grouped test partition (n=37,430; R²=0.711; RMSE=5.82 µg m⁻³; MAE=3.44 µg m⁻³). Its mean residual was 0.17 µg m⁻³. Performance exceeded both linear regression with the same predictors (R²=0.600; RMSE=6.85 µg m⁻³) and a Random Forest using fire-only PM₂.₅ alone (R²=0.592; RMSE=6.92 µg m⁻³). Across the five grouped cross-validation folds, R² averaged 0.581 (SD=0.081; range 0.453–0.647), RMSE averaged 6.19 µg m⁻³ (SD=0.63), and MAE averaged 3.41 µg m⁻³ (SD=0.07), indicating meaningful site-year heterogeneity.

Fire-only CMAQ PM₂.₅ was the dominant predictor in independent-test permutation analysis, followed by longitude, latitude, year, and day of year. Distance to fire and its missingness indicator contributed comparatively little conditional importance. The no-fire-component sensitivity model performed substantially worse (R²=0.335), supporting the greater diagnostic relevance of wildfire-attributable model loading while avoiding simultaneous use of the two components that reconstruct total CMAQ PM₂.₅.

Post-hoc regime evaluation showed R² values of 0.670 for background observations, 0.730 for moderate observations, and 0.695 for high observations. Absolute performance nevertheless deteriorated sharply in the high regime: among 473 test observations above 35 µg m⁻³, RMSE was 29.23 µg m⁻³ and MAE was 21.06 µg m⁻³. The mean predicted-minus-actual residual was +16.97 µg m⁻³, showing that the model tended to understate the magnitude of CMAQ underprediction during the highest observed concentrations.

Restoring the 18 quarantined high-CMAQ records without capping reduced test R² to 0.381 and increased RMSE to 9.44 µg m⁻³. This sensitivity demonstrates that the unverified upper-tail values materially affect squared-error performance and should remain transparently separated from the primary QC analysis until their raw-source provenance is confirmed.

## Discussion — interpretation and limitation

The revised analysis asks whether wildfire, spatial, and temporal context can explain CMAQ error without providing the model the two quantities that directly define that error. The persistence of substantial grouped holdout performance after removal of observed and total CMAQ PM₂.₅ indicates that the diagnostic signal is not solely an artifact of direct target leakage. Fire-only PM₂.₅ dominated the revised importance ranking, while spatial and temporal variables added information beyond fire loading alone.

These results should not be interpreted as an operational bias-correction product. Validation was internal to the available California monitoring record, performance varied among site-year folds, and absolute errors remained large under the highest observed PM₂.₅ conditions. External validation across different wildfire seasons, regions, model configurations, and emissions inventories would be required before predictive deployment. In addition, the 18 extreme CMAQ values require direct verification against the original model output; the present sensitivity analysis quantifies their influence but does not determine whether they are physically valid or erroneous.
